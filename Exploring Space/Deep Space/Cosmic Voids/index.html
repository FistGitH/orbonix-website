WHAT IS A COSMIC VOID?

A cosmic void is a huge region of the Universe where galaxies and matter are much less concentrated than average. They are not completely empty. Extremely thin gas, dark matter and occasional galaxies can still exist inside them. Voids are surrounded by denser structures such as galaxy filaments, sheets and clusters.

THE COSMIC WEB

On the largest scales, matter in the Universe forms a vast network known as the cosmic web. Galaxies gather along enormous filaments and walls, while huge empty regions form between them. Gravity gradually pulled matter toward denser regions, leaving some areas with far fewer galaxies.

ARE VOIDS REALLY EMPTY?

No. A cosmic void is not a perfect vacuum. It can contain extremely diffuse gas, dark matter and isolated galaxies. The important difference is density: there is dramatically less matter than in the surrounding cosmic structures.

HOW DID VOIDS FORM?

Tiny variations in the density of the early Universe grew under gravity. Matter flowed away from underdense regions and toward denser ones. Over billions of years, these differences became the enormous network of clusters, filaments and voids that we observe today.

ANCIENT STRUCTURE
Voids developed over billions of years as cosmic matter evolved under gravity.
COSMIC MAP
Mapping voids helps astronomers understand the large-scale evolution of the Universe.
DID YOU KNOW?
The Universe is not simply a collection of galaxies scattered randomly through space. On enormous scales, galaxies form a cosmic web — and the vast emptiness between its threads can be just as important as the galaxies themselves.
